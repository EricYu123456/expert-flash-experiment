Emerging technologies, such as Spin-Orbit Torque Magnetic Tunnel Junctions (SOT-MTJs), enable In-Memory Analog Computing (IMAC) architectures that offer substantial improvements in power efficiency and performance. However, Process Variation (PV) significantly impacts SOT-MTJ Magnetoresistive Random Access Memory (MRAM) performance by causing resistance shifts that induce bit-flips in memristive crossbar arrays, which results in degraded inference accuracy and reliability. In this work, we investigate the architectural-level impact of PV on inference accuracy and introduce a post-training weight mapping approach to mitigate faults in SOT-MRAM devices. We propose a Variation Impact Score (VIS) metric to quantify sub-array fault sensitivity and a Fault Observation Window (FOW) framework to localize vulnerable crossbar regions. Using SPICE-level evaluations, we demonstrate consistent accuracy recovery under severe process-induced faults, with recovery in early layers and substantial gains in the final decision layer, ensuring robust and reliable IMAC architectures despite hardware-induced bit-flips. Engineering Mathematics and computing Introduction In recent years, Deep Neural Networks (DNNs), such as Deep Learning Transformers, have demonstrated significant performance gains in various applications, including computer vision, natural language processing, robotics, and wearable biomedical monitoring 1 . However, DNN inference demands significant computational resources, intensive memory access, and communication bandwidth, while substantially increasing power consumption. The conventional von Neumann architecture, characterized by separate computing and memory units, faces significant bottlenecks due to frequent data transfer between processors (CPU/GPU/TPU) and memory units. The movement of data introduces considerable overhead, contributing to higher power consumption and diminished overall efficiency 2 . To reduce data movement and reduce energy, In-Memory Computing (IMC) has emerged as a promising solution which aims to blend computation and storage 3 . While much of the recent literature focuses on emerging non-volatile technologies, it is worth noting that the foundational concept of performing analog computation within memory arrays was established in pioneering works dating back decades, utilizing floating-gate and CMOS-based architectures 4 , 5 , 6 . Most IMC approaches accelerate the matrix-vector/matrix-matrix multiplication (MVM/MMM), which is the primary computational task of DNN inference. While these novel architectures showed promising improvement in performance, the security and trustworthiness often remain a secondary concern during development. Many early IMC implementations, particularly those based on standard CMOS technology, prioritised computational accuracy over robustness, leaving them with inherent vulnerabilities. A prime example of this trade-off is that while these CMOS-based systems reduce data transfer overhead, they simultaneously introduce significant static standby leakage currents required for data retention, which compromise data storage and increase vulnerability to threats such as power side-channel and fault injection attacks 7 . These challenges in CMOS-based systems have motivated a shift toward architectures built on emerging non-volatile memory (NVM) devices. While these NVMs can be used for both digital and analog IMC circuits, the analog IMC has garnered significant attention for its potential to achieve unparalleled energy efficiency 8 , 9 . This potential for unparalleled energy efficiency is primarily enabled by the integration of emerging non-volatile technologies, such as Magnetic Random Access Memory (MRAM) and Resistive RAM (RRAM), which serve as the foundation for high-performance In-Memory Analog Computing (IMAC) architectures 9 , 10 , 11 . Within this emerging landscape, particular attention has turned to Spin-Orbit Torque Magnetic RAM (SOT-MRAM), which is built upon the Spin-Orbit Torque Magnetic Tunnel Junction (SOT-MTJ) device. SOT-MTJ is a three-terminal device that effectively decouples the read and write paths. This significantly improves computing efficiency and reliability and makes SOT-MTJ a compelling choice for robust, energy-efficient, and high-performance IMAC architectures 10 , 12 . Building upon the robust device-level foundation of SOT-MRAM for high-performance IMAC, ensuring the security and trustworthiness of these architectures in practical deployment presents a distinct set of challenges. Globalized semiconductor manufacturing flows introduce opportunities for malicious modifications and unintentional process variations, both of which threaten the security and reliability of these systems 13 . With technological advancements in the semiconductor processing industry, the cost of maintaining and creating tools for Integrated Circuits (IC) manufacturing has increased rapidly. Thus, the majority of IC design companies have adopted a fabless model that utilizes a distributed global supply chain. This approach necessitates several distinct stages for the design, manufacturing, and validation of ICs and has resulted in the emergence of several hardware vulnerabilities and threats 13 , 14 . The current IC supply chain model largely developed around traditional CMOS-based designs allows adversaries to introduce malicious design modifications at various stages of the process, such as fault injection, side-channel attacks 15 , reverse engineering 16 , hardware Trojan 17 , and particularly Process Variations (PV) 18 during fabrication. Usually, PV is due to the non-idealities of the manufacturing process, such as oxide thickness and device dimensions variation, and it poses significant reliability and security risks 18 . We have previously demonstrated that variations in oxide thickness of an MTJ can introduce significant behavior shifts and result in unwanted bit-flips 18 . These bit-flips significantly affect the performance and reliability of the ML inference operation due to substantial accuracy degradation. Although prior research has underscored the importance of addressing such threats, there is a need for a solution to mitigate them properly. This research directly addresses this gap by introducing and thoroughly evaluating a novel weight mapping technique that dynamically allocates neural network weights to prioritize low-variation regions over high-variation regions within the DNN crossbar architecture. Our approach aims to enhance resilience against security threats while preserving inference accuracy. Additionally, our approach aims to achieve this goal without significantly altering the neural network architecture while not incurring excessive computational and area overheads. In this paper, we propose Dyn-A-Map, a novel Dynamic AI-model Mapping approach for robust MRAM-based IMAC hardware implementations. We demonstrate the advantage of Dyn-A-Map utilizing an IMAC-based presilicon simulation framework. In particular, we demonstrate the advantages of our proposed approach using the IMAC-Sim simulation framework 19 , 20 , which is a Python-based circuit-level simulator explicitly designed for the design space exploration of IMAC architectures. This tool enables the generation of SPICE netlists for IMAC circuits based on device- and circuit-level hyperparameters and evaluates the performance of these architectures with user-specified datasets. Figure 1 provides an overview of our proposed fault-aware IMAC framework. The process begins with the SOT-MTJ-based IMAC architecture, where fabrication-induced variations cause unwanted bit-flips in the IMAC array. The methodology then illustrates how Variation Impact Score (VIS) guides the Dyn-A-Map strategy to map weights from faulty processing elements to reliable ones using Fault Observation Windows (FOWs), thereby recovering inference accuracy. The major contributions of this paper are summarized as follows: 1. Utilizing the IMAC-Sim framework to systematically simulate and analyze bit-flip errors arising from realistic process variations in SOT-MRAM-based AI hardware accelerators. 2. Introducing and validating an algorithmic approach for identifying high-PV regions using a Variation Impact Score (VIS), which enables the ranking of sub-arrays based on their fault susceptibility. This facilitates informed decisions for mapping model parameters into low-PV, reliable regions to enhance robustness against random and targeted faults. 3. Building on this insight, we develop Dyn-A-Map, a dynamic weight mapping algorithm that mitigates the architectural-level impact of process variation-induced bit-flips. Dyn-A-Map enables significant recovery in inference accuracy by mapping critical weights to reliable memory regions. Results Impact of process variations on IMAC performance Monte Carlo-based accuracy degradation with uniform bit-flip injection We performed a comprehensive Monte Carlo analysis to quantify the impact of uniform bit-flip injections across all layers of the neural network. Each simulation consisted of 100 independent runs, where every run processed four randomly selected MNIST images, grouped into two batches, providing statistically robust insight into network performance under process variation. The baseline reference results run 1 without bit-flip injection to the weights, recorded a baseline accuracy of 75% and an average power consumption of 4.6678 W. From the simulation results, we observe an average inference power of ~ 4.67 W, which represents the sustained circuit power while executing DNN workloads. This reported power, obtained through full SPICE-level simulation, includes contributions from the memristive crossbars (SOT-MRAM arrays), analog neuron circuits, interconnect parasitics, and the additional partitioning and switching circuitry modeled in IMAC-Sim. Thus, the 4.67 W figure in the Dyn-A-Map study should be interpreted as the total average inference power consumption of the IMAC system, not per individual operation, but the combined instantaneous draw of all circuit components during active inference. Table 1 presents the comprehensive results across all fault rates, summarizing mean accuracy, standard deviation, and corresponding power consumption metrics for each bit-flip rate from 5% to 25%. The increasing bit-flip error rate results in a significant drop in average inference accuracy, from 66.25% at 5% fault rate to only 23.25% at 25% fault rate. Notably, the standard deviation of accuracy also decreases as error rates rise, which indicates a trend toward consistently poor performance under severe fault injection. Throughout these experiments, mean power consumption remains essentially unchanged, approximately 4.66 W, demonstrating that bit-flip-induced accuracy loss does not translate to a significant change in overall power consumption per inference. Moreover, the total area remains unchanged across all bit-flip error rates, as each simulation consistently maps the same number of synaptic elements and network parameters onto the IMAC hardware array. We measured the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for inference accuracy and power consumption across all error scenarios to evaluate the statistical reliability of our simulation results, as presented in Table 1 . In contrast, the 95% confidence intervals for power consumption remain exceptionally narrow, between ± 0.00036 W and ± 0.00066 W, highlighting the remarkable stability of power draw across all error rates. Together, these results demonstrate a clear decoupling between inference accuracy and energy consumption: even as functional performance deteriorates sharply under process variation, the overall power profile remains steady. It also implies that IMAC hardware resources are fully utilized regardless of bit-flip rate, and that the platform’s energy predictability is robust to significant PV. In mission-critical and energy constrained edge computations, such decoupling introduces reliability concerns, as stable power consumption can mask severe functional degradation, making power-based monitoring or side-channel defences ineffective. Layer-wise sensitivity analysis using asymmetric bit-flip combinations To investigate the relative contribution of individual layers to overall inference reliability, we performed a series of asymmetric bit-flip fault injections in the IMAC-based DNN architecture. Each experiment consisted of 100 Monte Carlo runs, where 20% error was injected into one or more layers according to the following layer configurations: ( L 1 : 20%; L 2 : 0%; L 3 : 0%), ( L 1 : 0%; L 2 : 20%; L 3 : 0%), ( L 1 : 0%; L 2 : 0%; L 3 : 20%), ( L 1 : 5%; L 2 : 10%; L 3 : 15%), ( L 1 : 15%; L 2 : 5%; L 3 : 10%), and ( L 1 : 20%; L 2 : 15%; L 3 : 10%). In each configuration, the first, second, and third values correspond to the bit-flip error rate applied to Layer 1, Layer 2, and Layer 3, respectively. The test set had four handwritten MNIST digits: 0, 1, 2, and 7. This reduced subset was selected to optimize simulation runtime across the 100 Monte Carlo runs, while still capturing diverse classification behaviors under faults. Fixing these digits across all experiments ensured that variations in accuracy were attributable solely to fault injection rather than dataset randomness. The digits were standardized throughout all trials to guarantee uniform assessment under various fault conditions. Figure 4 shows the mean accuracy for each layer configuration. When 20% errors are injected exclusively into Layer 1, the network incurs a small degradation in accuracy of 12.75%. This indicates that early feature extraction in Layer 1 has a comparatively lower susceptibility to this level of bit-flip perturbation. In contrast, injecting the same fault rate into Layer 3 alone results in a more severe accuracy drop of 32.25%. This mainly highlights the greater influence of late-stage layers on the final decision output of the model. Mixed error configurations across all the layers show the most significant degradation, with up to nearly 62% accuracy loss under the most aggressive injection profile ( L 1 : 20%; L 2 : 15%; L 3 : 10%), as faults propagate and compound across the entire computation pipeline. VIS-based layer fault sensitivity To capture PV impact at fine granularity before mapping, we compute a per-FOW sensitivity ranking for each layer and for each injected fault rate ρ ∈ {50, 60, 70, 80, 90}%. Consistent with the VIS protocol in Variation Impact Score Calculation subsection, every FOW is evaluated separately and over a single batch of 100 MNIST images so that the score reflects stable circuit behavior after the induced bit-flips. For a fixed fault rate ρ and layer ℓ , let \({E}_{i}^{(\rho )}\) denote the observed error rate (i.e., one minus accuracy over the 100 images) when only FOW i is injected with bit-flips. The Final VIS Rank first orders all FOWs by \({E}_{i}^{(\rho )}\) (higher error ⇒ worse rank). When two or more FOWs exhibit identical \({E}_{i}^{(\rho )}\) , we break the tie using the average-power deviation \(\delta {P}_{i}^{(\rho )}\triangleq | {P}_{i}^{(\rho )}-{P}_{base}| ,\) where \({P}_{i}^{(\rho )}\) is the measured mean power for FOW i at injection level ρ , and P base is the baseline “stock” mean power obtained from the same circuit with no injected faults, which was measured as 4.7W. The FOW with the larger \(\delta {P}_{i}^{(\rho )}\) is ranked worse. If a tie still persists, we use the FOW index in row-major order as a stable, deterministic tiebreaker. In IMAC crossbars operated with a fixed drive, instantaneous dynamic power is proportional to the sum of row currents, i.e., \(P\propto \sum {V}_{row}^{2}{G}_{ij}\) across active cells. Localized bit-flips alter the conductance pattern inside the stressed FOW, changing line currents and IR-drop distributions. Although the global mean power of the network stays consistent, these small but systematic deviations are layout and polarity-dependent and therefore informative of how strongly a given fault location perturbs analog current flow. Using \(\delta {P}_{i}^{(\rho )}\) as a second-order criterion (i) preserves determinism under equal error rates and (ii) preferentially flags FOWs that induce larger conduction imbalance as less robust. Impact of dynamic weight mapping We first obtained the VIS ranking for each layer under fault injection, considering error rates between 50% and 90% applied at the FOW granularity. In this subsection, we evaluate Dyn-A-Map by stressing each layer with localized bit-flip injections across this range and subsequently mapping weights using the VIS-driven swapping algorithm. As a reference, an error-free IMAC-Sim run over 100 images achieves 69% inference accuracy with an average power consumption of 4.7 W . This serves as the baseline accuracy and power for all subsequent comparisons. Fault injection at the FOW level reveals that the magnitude of accuracy degradation varies across layers. The VIS ranking consistently identifies the most error-prone FOWs at each injection rate, providing a foundation for analyzing why different layers exhibit distinct levels of resilience. For Layer 1, which contains 52 FOWs, the accuracy drop from the baseline remains modest even at high injection levels. Across 50-90% error injection, accuracy before mapping fluctuates around 61-62%, corresponding to only a 7–8% loss from the error-free case. For Layer 2, which comprises 12 FOWs, a slightly higher sensitivity is observed compared to Layer 1. Accuracy decreases from 61% at 50% injection to nearly 52% at 90% fault injection, amounting to a total loss of 17% relative to the baseline. In contrast, Layer 3, which contains only 3 FOWs and 10 neurons, exhibits the sharpest decline. Accuracy drops from 30% at 50% fault injection to just 17% at 90% fault injection, representing a catastrophic reduction of more than 50. Authors in ref. 21 demonstrate that faults in the last hidden and output layers of spiking neural networks are the most critical, while earlier layers remain largely resilient. Additionally, the work in ref. 22 demonstrates that strong regularization of feature extractors, achieved via an adversarial two-phase training framework, significantly improves fault tolerance. Turning to the mapping results, Fig. 6 contrasts accuracy before fault-aware mapping and after VIS-guided mapping for each layer under localized fault injection at rates of 50–90% applied at the FOW level. For reference, the error-free run without any fault injection achieves 69% accuracy, which is marked as the baseline in the figure. In Layer 1, accuracy without mapping consistently remains near 61–62% across all injection levels, but after mapping, the values rise to 65–68%, narrowing the gap toward the baseline. This modest recovery is expected, since the large fan-in of 400 × 120 synapses produces heavy line activity and distributes faults broadly. Layer 2 demonstrates stronger recovery. At high fault injection rates of 70–90%, accuracy improves from 52–57% without mapping and up to 61–63% with Dyn-A-Map, approaching within 8% of the error-free reference. An adverse case occurs in Layer 2 with a 50% fault injection rate, where accuracy drops from 61% to 58% after mapping. Here, the faulty FOW is 3 with geometry 32 × 20, an edge tile. The mapping rule allows swaps only between dimension-compatible FOWs. Thus, the only legal destinations were FOW 6 or FOW 9. From the VIS ranking at this fault rate, FOW 6 is ranked 10th, and FOW 9 is ranked 7th. Thus, the algorithm selected FOW 6, which is compatible, but not the lowest VIS among the compatible candidates, yielding a small negative swing. Such cases highlight how geometry constraints in edge partitions restrict mapping choices, occasionally resulting in non-ideal options when no strong partner exists. Finally, Layer 3, with only three FOWs of dimension 32 × 10 or 20 × 10, shows the sharpest improvements at higher error rates. In this instance, accuracy increases from 21% to 36% at 80% fault injection rate and from 17% to 34% at 90% fault injection rate, nearly doubling in each case. The improvement is attributed to the fact that FOW 1 was similarly defective in these runs. Hence, substituting the compromised area with FOW 1 resulted in more dependable mappings and enhanced recovery. The limited swap space implies that defective regions can only interact with one or two FOWs. Nonetheless, as faults in the final layer directly influence class determination boundaries, even a partial redistribution of essential weights results in significant noticeable improvements. This constrained FOW architecture presents a possible vulnerability, since adversaries may intentionally focus on these limited sub-arrays to amplify system-wide degradation. Table 3 summarizes the power profile stability and architectural impact of the VIS-guided FOW mapping across various layers and localized fault injection rates. To precisely characterize the relationship between the system states, we report the Pre-Map Deviation and Post-Map Deviation, which are calculated by subtracting the fixed 4.7 W architectural baseline from the measured pre-mapping and post-mapping power states, respectively. The Power Impact (mW) metric is subsequently introduced as the differential between the Post-Map and Pre-Map deviations, a method that explicitly isolates the net shift in the power profile caused by the remapping logic independent of the absolute deviation introduced by stochastic fault injection. A layer-wise comparison of these metrics reveals that Layer 1 exhibits the highest variance, with a Power Impact ranging from − 0.26 mW to + 0.16 mW. This behavior is expected, since Layer 1 has a much larger fan-in (400 × 120 synapses) and more variable switching activity, which introduces additional noise into its power profile. In contrast, Layers 2 and 3 demonstrate near-total stability, with the Power Impact remaining consistently within a negligible range of 0.00 mW to ± 0.01 mW regardless of the error rate. Ultimately, the results in Table 3 demonstrate that the Power Impact is consistently negligible across all layers, remaining bounded within ± 0.26 mW. This corresponds to approximately 0.006% of the total 4.7 W power envelope, a finding that is physically significant because it validates that Dyn-A-Map effectively decouples accuracy recovery from power consumption. In a security context, this decoupling ensures the system maintains intrinsic resilience against power side-channel analysis; because the mapping logic corrects inference errors without altering the total power draw, an attacker cannot distinguish between a faulty, unmapped state and a corrected, mapped state based on the power signature. Table 3 Summary of VIS-guided FOW mapping across layers and error rates with associated power deviation and impact analysis Full size table The table also reflects that VIS tends to redirect faulty regions toward border tiles or early-index FOWs, which typically contribute less to decision-critical activations, while preserving mid-array tiles that carry the majority of useful computation. In other words, the ranking ensures that the remapping takes advantage of available"slack” capacity in low-sensitivity regions rather than disturbing highly influential sub-arrays. Without such ranking, naive relocation strategies risk pushing weights into arbitrary or edge regions, potentially destabilizing the network. This need for precision becomes critical in adaptive DNN systems that employ RoI-centric or focus-based techniques, where careless swapping can backfire. For instance, Crop&Match deliberately performs RoI cropping and feature matching to amplify small, decision-critical regions 23 , while Foveated HDR pipelines allocate compute based on predicted user gaze but must remain model- and content-aware to avoid artifacts 24 . Thus, blindly pushing weights to extreme-edge FOWs or ad-hoc regions risks disrupting these semantics, whereas VIS provides the exact ranking under fault injection to pinpoint specific FOWs with lower importance in decision making. Beyond the evaluated network, the proposed Dyn-A-Map framework is inherently scalable to larger deep learning models typically deployed in real-world edge scenarios. Although SPICE-level simulations necessitate smaller datasets to maintain feasible runtimes, Algorithm 2 operates on FOWs rather than individual weights, meaning the complexity of ranking and swapping scales with the number of tiles rather than the total parameter count. Consequently, even for large-scale networks with millions of parameters, the number of FOWs remains within a computationally manageable range for standard edge controllers. Furthermore, Dyn-A-Map is designed as a static, offline calibration technique. In a practical edge deployment, this algorithm would be executed during the device’s initial boot-up or at scheduled maintenance intervals utilizing system-level diagnostic routines to generate the VIS profile. Once the optimal mapping is determined, it is frozen for runtime operations, thereby decoupling the calibration cost from active inference for resource-constrained edge platforms. Discussion This work presents a comprehensive pre-silicon methodology for quantifying and mitigating PV-induced bit-flip faults in SOT-MRAM-based IMAC architectures. It integrates SPICE-level accuracy with architecture-aware fault modeling to characterize the resilience limits of spintronic AI hardware. Through Monte Carlo simulations with 100 instances across a range of uniform and asymmetric bit-flip injection patterns, we demonstrate inference accuracy degradation with increasing fault rates. Our layer-wise sensitivity analysis further reveals that deeper layers exert a disproportionately large influence on final inference outcomes, with the output layer showing greater susceptibility to faults due to its direct role in defining decision boundaries. Building on these observations, our VIS-based analysis provides detailed visibility into fault sensitivity at the sub-array level in IMAC architecture. It produces ranked FOW lists and a heatmap that highlight which regions are most vulnerable under high-intensity fault injections. Utilizing this understanding, we executed VIS-guided dynamic weight mapping in IMAC-Sim, methodically mapping essential weights to more robust FOWs. Our evaluation shows that this technique reliably recovers inference accuracy and moves it closer to the baseline by leveraging available “slack” capacity in low-sensitivity regions. Crucially, while performing this remapping, we observed that the net power impact remains remarkably stable, with a deviation of only ± 0.26 mW in the most active layers. However, we observed that in the final layer, where only a limited number of FOWs exhibit dimensional inconsistencies, the mapping process is constrained with a decline in accuracy when no suitable swap partners exist. This creates a potential vulnerability point that adversaries could exploit to inject targeted faults and further reduce robustness. These findings indicate that VIS functions both as a diagnostic instrument for spatially identifying PV-prone areas and as a basis for an efficient mitigation strategy that mostly reinstates functional robustness without necessitating retraining. While fault-aware retraining could theoretically maximize robustness, it requires access to training datasets and back-propagation hardware that are typically unavailable in deployed edge environments. By avoiding these computational and data-centric overheads, our approach offers a viable zero-training alternative that restores reliability through spatial remapping alone. Together, VIS and Dyn-A-Map provide a pre-silicon, training-free mitigation that restores a substantial fraction of lost accuracy under severe localized faults. The proposed Dyn-A-Map approach establishes an important pathway for mitigating reliability issues stemming from PV in SOT-MRAM-based IMAC accelerators. However, several promising avenues remain for future research. One immediate direction involves fine-grained fault tracking and logging mechanisms, specifically tracking accessed weights such as row and column indices during each inference, and mapping them against a pre-established fault map. The stability of the power profile during mapping further suggests that Dyn-A-Map can maintain resilience against power-based side-channel analysis, as the mapping ensures accuracy improvement without creating a distinguishable power signature. The incorporation of the VIS into fault tolerance co-design approaches offers significant prospects. VIS-aware and fault-resilient mapping into the training phase, such as selective pruning, quantization, and retraining, offers the potential to proactively decrease fault susceptibility while boosting overall robustness. To determine practical feasibility, hardware prototyping and silicon-level experimentation are critical subsequent steps. Creating and evaluating physical SOT-MRAM-based IMAC accelerator prototypes would yield essential insights into actual operational dynamics, confirm simulation-derived results, and underscore further considerations required for implementation. The proposed Dyn-A-Map methodology and VIS-aware mapping technique are transferable to other emerging memristive non-volatile memory technologies, such as ReRAM, PCM, and FeFET, which provides the opportunity for wider adoption in the domain of resilient, trustworthy, and secure analog computing. Methods Background and related work The study of PV impact on nanoscale devices such as SOT-MTJ is crucial for improving the reliability and efficiency of IMAC architectures. In this section, an overview of IMAC and its role in deep learning acceleration is provided, followed by a discussion on SOT-MTJ devices, their benefits, and the challenges associated with them. Next, we examine PV and its effects on system performance, and explore weight mapping techniques as a potential solution to mitigate PV impact and offer robustness. In-memory analog computing Within the broader field of IMC, these architectures are typically classified as either digital or analog IMCs. Digital IMC performs Boolean logic directly within memory arrays, often leveraging established technologies such as SRAM 25 . This work, however, focuses on In-Memory Analog Computing (IMAC), which utilizes the physical device properties of non-volatile memories (SOT-MRAM) to execute large-scale matrix computations, such as Matrix Multiplication (MatMul), with massive parallelism. Even when accounting for the significant energy overhead of the required Analog-to-Digital and Digital-to-Analog Converters (ADCs/DACs), the massive reduction in core computational energy allows IMAC to achieve a lower total energy per operation compared to digital IMCs 2 , 26 , 27 . This potential for unparalleled energy efficiency in AI acceleration has made analog IMC approach a significant and promising area of research, which this section will explore in further detail. Among various IMAC circuit-level simulation frameworks, IMAC-Sim has gained considerable attention due to its ability to efficiently model MatMul, which is the fundamental operation in DNNs, utilizing crossbar arrays consisting of memristive architectures 19 , 28 . Unlike conventional circuit-level simulation frameworks such as DPE 29 and CCCS 30 , IMAC-Sim uniquely provides full SPICE-level modeling for analog IMC circuits and supports automatic calculation of interconnect parasitics based on physical layout and device parameters. Furthermore, it implements both horizontal and vertical partitioning techniques to alleviate the impact of parasitics on large-scale arrays, and simultaneously reports DNN inference accuracy, power consumption, and latency. These capabilities make IMAC-Sim a powerful tool for realistic and accurate design space exploration of IMAC hardware. To understand this further, IMAC architectures are based solely on crossbar arrays, where memory cells are programmed with conductance values representing neural network weights. Input voltages are applied to the rows of the crossbar, and then the resulting current flow through the columns naturally computes the weighted sum of the inputs based on Ohm’s Law and Kirchhoff’s Current Law. It realizes MatMul operations in a single computational step 20 . The IMAC architecture leverages memory crossbars to execute MatMul operations in parallel, thereby significantly improving computational throughput and energy-efficiency. Unlike CPUs, which require multiple clock cycles to perform multiply-and-accumulate (MAC) operations sequentially, IMAC executes these computations in a single step inside the crossbar array structures. This approach reduces computational latency and power consumption and makes it an attractive solution for energy-efficient AI acceleration, particularly for edge applications where hardware resources are constrained 20 . To further enhance the robustness of IMAC architectures, researchers have explored the integration of NVM technologies within crossbar arrays. This includes RRAM, Phase-Change Memory (PCM), and MRAM 18 , 20 . These NVM devices store data as resistance states, allowing for weight storage and computation within the same physical unit. Although IMAC scales well for deep learning workloads since weight storage and computation are integrated into a single unit inside the arrays, it can suffer from implementation issues, such as fault injection, bit flip attack, PV, device aging, and parasitic effects that degrade computational accuracy over time 18 . Expanding on these implementation issues, the non-ideal behavior of the crossbar arrays presents a fundamental challenge to the reliability of IMAC systems. The core problem is not in maintaining non-volatility, but rather in preserving the robustness of the analog conductance states that represent the DNN weights and biases. As these arrays scale to larger dimensions, several physical phenomena degrade computational accuracy. For instance, parasitic wire resistance accumulates along the word and bit-lines, causing significant IR-drops that distort the delicate analog signals and reduce the read margin. A primary strategy to address hardware-level reliability issues is crossbar partitioning, as introduced in IMAC-Sim, a method that divides the large crossbar into a tiled configuration of smaller sub-arrays 20 . Partitioning efficiently diminishes those adverse impacts of parasitic resistance and enhances overall signal integrity by minimising the physical length of the interconnects. Spin-orbit torque magnetic tunnel junctions (SOT-MTJs) In recent years, there has been a growing interest in spintronics-based devices as researchers seek energy-efficient alternatives to meet the rising demands of parallel processing 31 . Particularly, Magnetic Tunnel Junctions (MTJs) have gained considerable attention due to their potential in high-speed memory and logic applications 18 . Despite the commercial presence of Spin-Transfer Torque (STT)-based MTJs, which utilize a two-terminal structure, they suffer from inherent limitations such as long incubation delays and a high read failure. These issues are largely due to the shared read-and-write path, which introduces bit-flips and read disturbance. To overcome these limitations, Spin-Orbit Torque (SOT)-based MTJ devices have emerged as a compelling alternative. Unlike STT-MRAM, SOT-MRAM is structured as a three-terminal device, where the read and write operations are physically decoupled, and this separation is achieved by integrating an additional heavy metal (HM) layer beneath the MTJ stack. As a result, SOT-MTJs offer reduced write latency with lower bit error rates and overall improved energy-efficiency compared to their STT counterparts. To contextualize the selection of SOT-MRAM for this architecture, it is essential to compare its properties with other Compute-in-Memory (CIM) primitives. While weight mapping algorithms can technically be applied to technologies such as ReRAM, Floating-Gate (FG), and Ferroelectric FET (FeFET), these devices are often constrained by limited write endurance 20 , 32 , 33 , 34 . This limitation is typically orders of magnitude lower than that of spintronic devices, making frequent post-deployment mapping hazardous to hardware longevity. Furthermore, low endurance introduces a potential vulnerability to endurance exhaustion attacks where an adversary could force repeated updates to accelerate device degradation. SOT-MRAM, however, uniquely combines the non-volatility of resistive memory with virtually infinite endurance ( > 10 15 cycles) and sub-nanosecond switching speeds. These characteristics make it the optimal candidate for the Dyn-A-Map framework, enabling a robust architectural mitigation strategy that can sustain high-frequency reconfigurations in response to active threats without compromising device lifespan. A typical SOT-MTJ device comprises an MTJ, consisting of an oxide layer between two ferromagnetic layers placed on top of the heavy metal, as illustrated in Fig. 7 . The logical state of the MTJ, which is 0 or 1, mainly depends on the relative orientation of the free layer’s magnetization with respect to the pinned layer. A parallel alignment corresponds to a low-resistance state or ‘0’, whereas an anti-parallel alignment represents a high-resistance state or ‘1’. The behavior of these resistance states, Parallel ( R P ) and Anti-Parallel ( R A P ), can be modeled mathematically using the Tunnel Magnetoresistance (TMR) ratio and the fundamental MTJ resistance ( R M T J ) 18 : $${R}_{MTJ}=\frac{{T}_{ox}}{f\times A\times \sqrt{\phi }}\exp (1.025{T}_{ox}\sqrt{\phi })$$ (1) $$TMR=\frac{TM{R}_{0}}{1+{\left(\frac{{V}_{bias}}{{V}_{h}}\right)}^{2}}$$ (2) where R P = R M T J and R A P = R M T J (1 + T M R ). In these equations, T o x is the oxide layer thickness, f is a material-dependent parameter related to the resistance-area product, A is the device’s surface area, ϕ is the energy barrier height of the oxide layer, V b i a s is the bias voltage, and V h is the voltage at which the TMR drops to half of its initial value, T M R 0 . These equations are fundamental to our work, as they mathematically link physical manufacturing parameters such as T o x to the device’s electrical behavior. Specifically, as demonstrated in our prior analysis 18 , the exponential sensitivity of R M T J to T o x variations (Eq. ( 1 )) can significantly degrade the read noise margin. This physical vulnerability allows adversaries to exploit process-induced weak cells to trigger targeted bit-flips during inference, thereby compromising the system’s reliability and security. The functionality of the SOT-MTJ is realized through distinct read and write operations, both of which depend on the stability and the sensing margin between the resistance states defined by these physical parameters. To perform a write operation, a charge current is applied between terminals T2 and T3, which generates a spin current in the z-axis due to spin-orbit coupling. This spin current, oriented perpendicularly to the charge current, induces a torque that alters the magnetization of the free layer. The dynamic behavior of this magnetization switching is governed by the Landau-Lifshitz-Gilbert (LLG) equation, which models the precessional motion of the magnetization vector ( m ) under the influence of various torques 35 , 36 . This foundational equation is extended for spintronic devices to include the spin-torque ( T s ) from the SOT current, as shown in Equation ( 3 ): $$\frac{\partial {\bf{m}}}{\partial t}=-\gamma {\mu }_{0}{\bf{m}}\times {{\bf{H}}}_{e\mathrm{ff}}+\alpha {\bf{m}}\times \frac{\partial {\bf{m}}}{\partial t}+\frac{1}{{M}_{s}}{{\bf{T}}}_{s}$$ (3) where γ is the gyromagnetic ratio, μ 0 is the vacuum permeability, H eff is the effective magnetic field experienced by the magnetization of the free layer, α is the Gilbert damping parameter, and M s is the saturation magnetization. Understanding this equation is crucial as it links the applied currents to the precise magnetic dynamics that determine the device’s switching speed and reliability. For the read operation, a separate current path is established between terminals T1 and T2, allowing the stored state to be sensed without disturbing the state of the MTJ. This structural decoupling ensures enhanced read reliability and enables concurrent read-write operations. In this work, we adopt the modeling approach described in ref. 18 , where the dynamic behavior of SOT-MTJ devices is captured using a Verilog-AMS model grounded in physical equations. This paper demonstrates manufacturing PV in these physical parameters leads to unintended shifts in the R P and R A P values. Furthermore, the fault model employed in this work is directly grounded in the specific physical parameters of SOT-MRAM such as T o x variations, ensuring that the simulated bit-flips accurately reflect realistic device-level failure probabilities rather than arbitrary random noise. These resistance shifts are the root cause of the bit-flip vulnerabilities that degrade DNN accuracy. Process variations in nanoscale devices Process variations (PV) in modern silicon fabrication are well-known parameters, and are a critical aspect to consider for reliability analysis. In particular, for IMAC platforms, as the technology node shrinks below the 20nm scale, new challenges arise as fluctuations in lithographic precision, etching depth, and material uniformity become increasingly dominant. These differences cause noticeable changes in how the device works while it degrades at a system level unpredictably. For SOT-MTJ-based architectures, PV manifests in both device-level magnetic properties and interconnect-level parasitic behaviors. The results of the simulation at the device-level exhibit stochastic changes in the dimensions of the free layer, the thickness of the oxide tunnel barrier, and the roughness of the surface, which directly affect the resistive behavior between the parallel (P) and antiparallel (AP) states 18 . Previously 18 we evaluated the impact of PV on MTJ devices using a series of simulations under isolated and combined variation conditions across critical physical parameters. The parameters include oxide thickness (T ox ), free-layer width (W FL ), and length (L FL ). The results, based on 2000 Monte Carlo iterations, demonstrate that variations in W FL and L FL proportionally affect both R P and R AP while preserving the TMR ratio. However, the T ox exhibits exponential influence, with values below 1.1 nm triggering read currents (I read ) that exceed the critical switching threshold (I crit_sw ). This leads to unintentional AP-to-P transitions within the read window. In contrast, when T ox is maintained above 1.1nm, the device maintains its resistive state during reading while preserving logic stability. Combined variations constrained within ± 10% of nominal dimensions result in overlapping resistance distributions and anomalous outliers, which attackers can exploit to induce controlled bit-flips. In practice, resistance outliers may arise not only from benign manufacturing variability but also from adversarial supply-chain manipulation. In analog IMC architectures, reverse engineering can enable attackers to identify the physical locations of critical weights and selectively induce localized parametric perturbations effectively realizing targeted fault injection without modifying circuit logic. Consequently, the resistance-deviation model adopted in this section captures the dominant physical mechanism underlying both natural process variation and adversarial parametric attacks. These deviations reduce the sensing margin of the MTJ cell, which may propagate through the crossbar accelerator, further degrading the accuracy at the application-level. Particularly, the evaluation indicates that a global modification of T ox within less than 0.1nm can degrade classification accuracy by nearly 20% with only 0.05% random bit-flips in crossbar weights of a trained DNN model. These results underscore the critical role of physical parameter stability in maintaining robust computation in SOT-MTJ-based IMAC architectures. Beyond the device level, the interconnect parasitics introduced by back-end-of-line (BEOL) variations significantly degrade the signal propagation in analog crossbar arrays. The authors in ref. 20 demonstrated parasitic resistance and capacitance to grow non-linearly with metal scaling due to increased surface and grain boundary scattering effects. These effects are captured using the Fuchs-Sondheimer model for surface roughness and the Mayadas-Shatzkes model for grain boundary reflection. These models are parameterized in the IMAC-Sim framework to reflect interconnect degradation in different partition sizes and technology nodes. Moreover, analog IMC circuits often map signed weights onto differential conductance pairs. Because of the unwanted photovoltaic power at the device level, there is a presence of resistance drift, and the ratio between positive and negative conductance deviates from the ideal value. Due to this distortion, the resulting voltage of the dot product changes, and neurons show erroneous activations. In extreme cases, the imbalance introduces weight sign inversion, which results in unwanted bit-flips. We adopt this abstraction as our fault model to apply controlled variations and simulate the degradation of inference accuracy, and evaluate various severity levels. In this work, we incorporate variation-induced bit-flip behavior by introducing random-polarity errors in the weight matrix. This allows us to simulate real-world reliability degradation without requiring full transient magnetic switching simulations. Our approach is based on the variation modeling methodology proposed in ref. 18 , where the switching probability distributions were profiled between different SOT-MTJ manufacturing parameters to guide the tuning of the variation. Parameter mapping techniques Weight mapping refers to the process of translating trained neural network weights into physical representations compatible with the underlying hardware. In analog IMC systems, the digital-to-physical translation of DNN weights plays a crucial role in determining the computational accuracy and robustness of the entire architecture. For analog IMC, the weights are directly encoded into the conductance states of the resistive memory devices, such as ReRAM, PCM, or MRAMs. These conductance values form the basis of the computational foundation of IMAC architectures by determining how input voltages are converted into current outputs during MatMul 3 . For ensuring the correct output of these DNN models, each trained weight must be mapped carefully to an appropriate and distinguishable conductance level within the device’s physical range. DNN weights are typically split into two components: a positive and a negative branch, which are often realized through differential conductance pairs. This approach is often used to represent signed values and suppress linearity errors in analog signal flow in the architecture. The precision for mapping here is constrained by the device’s resistance variability, noise margin, and retention characteristics. In addition, the process is complicated by non-idealities of the large memory arrays, such as parasitic resistance, line resistance, and inter-device variation. Inaccurate mapping can lead to degraded signal-to-noise ratios and miscalculations of dot-product values, which ultimately reduce the inference accuracy. The drift in resistance may cause a bit-flip in the sign bit of the weight parameter, which results in incorrect neural activations. Recent studies highlight that sign-bit flips are especially critical, as adversaries can deliberately exploit the Most Significant Bit (MSB) through Bit-Flip Attacks (BFAs) to rapidly collapse model accuracy 37 . To systematically evaluate these resilience challenges and investigate effective hardware-level countermeasures, a reliable simulation environment is essential. In this work, we adopt the IMAC-Sim simulation framework 19 , a full SPICE-level modeling of analog IMC circuits and supports realistic weight mapping analysis under PVs. The mapping technique here is realized by translating each ± 1 weight parameter into two resistive paths using predefined R l o w and R h i g h values. A weight of + 1 is mapped to low resistance in the positive branch and high resistance in the negative branch, while a − 1 uses the reverse configuration. Biases are handled similarly in this framework while the final SPICE-compatible netlists are generated for circuit-level simulation and then tested with proper data to ensure its accuracy. This mapping strategy assumes perfect stability of resistance states and symmetric response from all memory cells, which is not realistic under PV assumptions outlined herein. This improper weight mapping poses both functional and security risks to the DNN architecture. If a critical weight of the trained DNN model is placed into a region of the crossbar that suffers from high variation, then it can lead to degradation in the output current. Additionally, it can expose the system to different threats such as side-channel leakage and fault injection vulnerabilities. The IMAC architecture performs MatMul in parallel across thousands of weights and it is highly sensitive to these disruptions. Therefore, careful placement of weights becomes essential for preserving accuracy while ensuring resiliency. To address these concerns, dynamic weight mapping has emerged as a promising solution. The dynamic weight mapping ensures the post-training reassignment of weight positions within a neural network crossbar array to mitigate hardware faults along with improving energy-efficiency and enhance resistance to adversarial conditions. Unlike retraining, the dynamic mapping does not modify the model structure, leading to learnable parameters. Instead, it strategically alters the spatial deployment of weights across the MRAM hardware architecture. Moreover, dynamic mapping can involve polarity adjustments, resistance window adaptation, and dynamic scaling based on fault maps. Prior works in this area have explored several strategies to improve resilience in analog neural accelerators under device faults. These include software-level approaches such as FAT-RABBIT, which enhances robustness by injecting bit-flips during training and redistributing weight importance to reduce sensitivity to faults 37 . Fault-Aware Training (FAT) schemes address this issue in the pre-silicon stage by hardening models during training, reducing their sensitivity to such critical bit-flips. In contrast, our work focuses on post-training, hardware-level remapping strategies, which directly enhance the resilience of deployed IMAC systems against variation-induced faults and adversarial manipulations. The authors in ref. 38 proposed a differential weight mapping technique for ReRAM-based DNNs that increases tolerance to stuck-at-fault defects without requiring additional hardware overhead. The proposed method selectively remaps critical weights using polarity-aware allocation, which significantly improves classification accuracy under high fault injection rates. The research work in ref. 39 introduced an efficient grouping-based remapping scheme where faulty cells are dynamically bypassed and the weight values are redistributed across more reliable crossbar regions in the architecture. This approach minimizes the latency and power overhead while avoiding the need for retraining the model. Xia et al. 40 introduced a fault-tolerant training framework for RRAM-based computing systems. They proposed a remapping scheme that leverages network sparsity to assign zero weights to faulty memory cells. Their neuron-reordering method minimizes the mismatch between fault maps and weight distribution. This allows faulty cells to be systematically bypassed without retraining the network. All these prior works are evidence of the growing importance of weight mapping as a lightweight yet effective approach to mitigate the impact of hardware faults in analog neural accelerators. The core principles in this weight mapping indicate fault-aware relocation, polarity balancing, and structural bypassing. These are equally applicable to any resistive memory technologies, such as SOT-MTJ. As shown in Table 4 , most existing IMC-oriented weight mapping strategies have been developed in the context of ReRAM-based systems and primarily focus on stuck-at faults or static fault models, or training-time robustness enhancements. While recent offline mapping approaches also improve resilience to static Stuck-At-Faults (SAFs) through grouping or hybrid remapping, these approaches remain limited to device-static fault assumptions and do not capture the dynamic, PV-induced bit-flips and spatially localized reliability variations that become critical in SOT-MRAM-based IMAC architectures. Our work addresses this gap by introducing Dyn-A-Map, a post-training, VIS-guided weight remapping strategy at the FOW granularity, explicitly designed to improve post-deployment robustness of SOT-MRAM-based IMAC architectures under PV-induced, spatially localized faults. Our methodology begins with an Adversarial Threat Model, wherein we carefully investigate all potential attack scenarios through which an adversary might introduce flaws into the IMAC hardware. After this threat modeling, we present the IMAC-Sim framework, a SPICE-based simulation tool designed to accurately replicate crossbar-level analog matrix-vector multiplications in MRAM-based IMAC systems. Adversarial threat model and attack scenarios To properly assess the security of SOT-MRAM-based IMAC designs, we must first define the potential attack vectors and adversarial capabilities. This foundational analysis will precede our systematic exploration of fault modeling and process variation. To accurately identify hardware vulnerabilities that may occur in the practical implementation of SOT-MRAM-based IMAC designs, we employ a gray-box adversarial model. We assume that an adversary in the semiconductor production and supply chain possesses privileged yet limited visibility into the IMAC device design, its designated neural network workload, and the physical allocation of logical weights to hardware arrays. The attacker can manipulate the fabrication process, intentionally introducing defects to diminish inference accuracy and overall system reliability. Furthermore, we assume that an adversary may be able to induce faults using optical fault injection, and row-hammer attacks, which cause bit-flips, are another common attack pattern. Within this threat model, we delineate the subsequent principal attack vectors: Malicious Insider Manipulation: An adversary within the chip design, fabrication, or packaging phases exploits their role to deliberately alter essential fabrication parameters, like oxide thickness or free-layer dimensions of SOT-MTJ devices 18 . These nuanced yet calculated modifications may lead to substantial bit-flip errors that disproportionately influence critical areas of the neural network. Reverse Engineering for Targeted Fault Injection: Adversaries can reverse engineer the hardware layout or the deployed IMAC chip to precisely identify the mapping of critical neural network weights onto physical crossbar sub-arrays. With this knowledge, the attackers can perform targeted insertion of bit-flips and create focused disruptions in the neural network’s critical weights. Side-Channel Attacks Amplified by Induced Faults: Furthermore, the attacker can leverage intrinsic side-channel leakage of information, which includes electromagnetic emissions or power consumption patterns. Such attacks further compromise device security by allowing leakage of sensitive model information, even the extraction of confidential model parameters. Crucially, while the physical mechanisms of these attacks differ, their functional impact on the memory array is identical. Whether originating from optical injection or fabrication tampering, these threats result in the corruption of stored weight values that manifest as bit-flips. Therefore, by modeling and mitigating extensive bit-flip errors, our framework provides a unified defense. This strategy effectively addresses both natural process variations and targeted adversarial threats, regardless of whether the faults are randomly distributed or spatially clustered. Given the opacity of the globalized semiconductor supply chain, system designers must assume that hardware may be affected by malicious interdiction, inherent process variation, or long-term aging effects. Consequently, our framework places the locus of defense at the architectural level, enabling designers to detect and mitigate these faults regardless of their origin, without requiring additional trust assumptions or modifications to supplier workflows. In mission-critical edge applications such as autonomous defense systems or healthcare monitoring such targeted degradation can lead to catastrophic failure, where even minor accuracy losses induced by specific weight corruptions compromise the safety and integrity of the deployment. Furthermore, our threat model clearly addresses a realistic post-production scenario in which PV-induced challenges manifest only after the IMAC device has been shipped and deployed, and the manufactured chip demonstrates correct functionality, meeting the design specs. In these circumstances, systematic testing and field-level diagnostics can reveal significant inference accuracy loss. To proactively mitigate these complex vulnerabilities, our strategy employs the VIS to assess susceptibility to faults at the sub-array level, facilitating dynamic mapping of neural network weights after construction. Overview of IMAC-Sim To build upon the modeling guidelines developed in IMAC-Sim 19 and the parasitic-aware crossbar partitioning methods outlined in Xbar-Partitioning 20 , this work leverages the circuit-level simulation framework to run and evaluate the proposed weight mapping and fault-aware parameter allocation strategies. Running on IMAC-Sim produces the corresponding SPICE netlists for each layer in a DNN, leveraging realistic physics-based modeling for capturing the device behavior for synapses and neurons. The analog neuron circuits employed in this framework are based on the SOT-MRAM sigmoid design validated in ref. 10 . Unlike mixed-signal architectures that require digital conversion at every layer, this fully-analog implementation performs activation directly on the accumulated crossbar currents, thereby eliminating the latency and power overhead of intermediate ADCs and DACs. It incorporates various user-defined hyperparameters, such as neuron types, device resistances ( R l o w , R h i g h ), synaptic cell dimensions, and interconnect properties (e.g., resistivity, width, and thickness), and generates the resulting inference accuracy, power consumption, and latency via circuit-level simulation. For experimental evaluation, the framework utilizes a three-layer fully-connected Multilayer Perceptron (MLP) with a 400 × 120 × 84 × 10 topology, trained on 20 × 20 pixel MNIST images. Weights are quantized to 1-bit binary values ({ − 1, + 1}) and physically mapped to differential SOT-MRAM resistance pairs ( R P , R A P ). Similarly, input activations are quantized to 1-bit binary levels ({ − 1, + 1}) via the sign function and encoded as bipolar voltages ( ± V D D ), while inter-layer activations utilize continuous analog sigmoid functions implemented via memristive voltage dividers. A key aspect of IMAC-Sim is its capability to model the interconnect parasitics using the physics equations. The resistance and capacitance are based on geometric and material properties, where nanoscale effects are addressed by using the Fuchs-Sondheimer and Mayadas-Shatzkes models for resistivity, and the Sakurai-Tamaru formulation for capacitance. Introduced voltage drops and signal delay, these parasitics can severely reduce the accuracy and energy-efficiency, especially in the large crossbar arrays. To overcome these limitations, IMAC-Sim incorporates both horizontal and vertical partitioning, a concept initially formalized in the Xbar-Partitioning framework 20 . One method to reduce the physical dimensions of a crossbar is to implement partitioning, which divides a large crossbar into smaller and easier-to-control sub-arrays. Thus, partitioning reduces the interconnect lengths and minimizes parasitic resistance and capacitance. User-defined horizontal and vertical partitioning vectors separate the weight matrix of each DNN layer into tiles. The same partitioning method is applied uniformly to all sub-arrays, and their outputs are then combined through peripheral circuits. The IMAC-Sim toolchain consists of three core Python modules: testIMAC , mapIMAC , and mapLayer . The simulation flow starts with the testIMAC module, which takes user-defined parameters such as: (i) trained weight and bias matrices, (ii) network topology, (iii) test datasets, and (iv) circuit-level parameters (neuron model, synapse resistance values, and device cell dimensions). For scalable execution of DNN models, testIMAC supports both horizontal and vertical partitioning of large crossbars driven through the H p and V p vectors. The mapIMAC module orchestrates circuit generation by calling mapLayer , which generates SPICE subcircuits for each layer. The layout-dependent parasitic resistance and capacitance are integrated into the crossbar partitions’ subcircuits. Technology-dependent parameters, e.g., resistivity, interconnect width, and metal thickness, can be dynamically defined after the calculation of interconnect parasitics. We obtain the resistive and capacitive parasitic models from Fuchs-Sondheimer and Mayadas-Shatzkes formulations, and Sakurai-Tamaru expressions, respectively. After constructing the netlist, IMAC-Sim performs simulations using HSPICE with piecewise-linear sources calculated from the input datasets. For this study, the MNIST dataset is used to evaluate pattern recognition capabilities for handwritten digits, with each input image injected as a piecewise-linear voltage source into the generated circuit. The simulation framework allows multiple test cases per batch. It is important to note that the scale of the transient simulations presented in this work is bounded by the numerical convergence constraints of standard SPICE solvers when handling massive non-linear matrices. To maintain physical fidelity, we utilize a tile-based modeling approach from Xbar-Partitioning 20 that explicitly characterizes parasitic and noise effects at the sub-array level. The simulation framework is designed to process multiple test cases per batch. In this evaluation, we utilized both a focused subset of 4 images and a broader set of 100 images sampled from the full 10 class MNIST dataset, employing batch sizes of 2 and 1, respectively. To test the fault injection model and the outcomes of these experiments, in every run, the same weight configuration has been used. The anticipated output voltages at the last layer are subsequently analyzed against the actual test labels to determine classification accuracy and to pinpoint misclassifications. This approach is particularly important as we introduce bit-flip faults into the weights, making it essential to test a larger number of cases to accurately capture error-prone regions within the crossbar arrays. Although running more test cases per batch increases the overall simulation time, it improves the robustness of error detection and further highlights the sensitivity of the architecture to hardware-induced faults. Additionally, the average power and latency metrics are extracted to evaluate energy efficiency under varying fault conditions. IMAC-Sim provides a comprehensive simulation pipeline suitable for early-stage co-design of devices, circuits, and learning models operating under non-ideal conditions, filling a critical void in the hardware-software performance modeling landscape. Fault injection modeling To evaluate the robustness of SOT-MRAM-based IMAC architectures under realistic hardware conditions, we introduce a detailed PV modeling approach within the IMAC-Sim simulation framework. At the device level, PV in SOT-MRAM, such as deviations in oxide thickness, free layer dimensions, and interface roughness, can cause stochastic fluctuations in resistance states. These fluctuations result in inadvertent bit-flips in stored neural network weights, compromising the precision and reliability of inference operations. While advanced process control in modern fabrication facilities minimizes global parameter deviations, this work intentionally simulates elevated fault rates to rigorously evaluate architectural robustness under worst-case operational conditions rather than nominal manufacturing yield. These scenarios account for irreducible local stochasticity in aggressively scaled devices and the reduced read noise margins inherent to low-voltage edge inference. Furthermore, the fault model encompasses post-deployment reliability threats, including Time-Dependent Dielectric Breakdown (TDDB), as well as localized adversarial manipulation of fabrication parameters (e.g., subtle oxide thickness perturbations introduced in confined regions), which can induce concentrated error densities that disproportionately impact inference accuracy 18 , 41 . By validating performance under these severe yet plausible fault conditions, we characterize the resilience limits of the IMAC architecture against long-term physical degradation and targeted weight-corruption scenarios. Our simulation methodology simulates device-level failures at the architecture and application levels by incorporating random bit-flips into the allocated weights. We incorporate a specialized fault injection technique into the current mapLayer module of the IMAC-Sim framework. This enables systematic fault injection during the pre-SPICE phase, allowing for a thorough assessment of their effects on system accuracy and power consumption. The following subsections clearly detail our methodology, from modeling the faults and evaluating their impact on inference accuracy, to the specific modifications made within IMAC-Sim and the definition and calculation of our proposed VIS metric. Bit-flip injection model Our approach for modeling PV-induced faults focuses on systematically introducing random bit-flips into the memristive crossbar arrays. Our major objective, as outlined in the threat model is to identify vulnerable regions within the crossbar arrays due to fabrication-induced PV, which manifest at the device level as unintended changes in MRAM device resistance values. Deviations in physical characteristics, including oxide thickness, magnetic anisotropy, and free-layer dimensions in SOT-MTJ devices, lead to substantial variability in resistance states, directly resulting in bit-flip errors. In a practical chip, each MRAM device generally displays minor discrepancies in these essential characteristics, leading to continuous and statistically distributed resistance variations 42 . As explained in our threat model, our main objective is to find the vulnerable regions. The fault injection procedure commences with the pre-silicon SPICE-level circuit modeling. The weights of the neural network, initially trained and optimized at the software level, are subsequently translated into resistance values within crossbar arrays. In our IMAC-Sim architecture, this is executed utilizing low-resistance (5k Ω ) and high-resistance (15k Ω ) states that correspond to the stored weights. Our fault injection module integrated into the new mapLayer.py script introduces controlled, randomized bit-flip errors directly into these resistances, thus altering their state from high to low resistance or vice versa. This modification closely mimics physical-layer PV-induced faults. The visual overview in Fig. 8 reinforces this procedure, where randomized bit-flip faults are introduced into mapped crossbar arrays across all layers. Fig. 8: An overview of the fault injection methodology. Full size image Device-level vulnerabilities stemming from PV in SOT-MRAM cells cause bit-flips in the hardware crossbar arrays. These randomized bit-flips are injected into targeted DNN layers, leading to a degradation in the model’s overall inference accuracy. In order to include a variety of fault injection scenarios for a comprehensive analysis, we chose fault rates of 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% in our evaluation. To obtain statistically meaningful results, these bit-flip errors were spread across each neural network layer, emulating realistic conditions where PV-induced faults may arise randomly throughout the crossbar without preferential clustering in the SPICE files layer<number>.sp . To ensure reliable results, we executed comprehensive Monte Carlo simulations with 100 runs for each of the predefined global bit-flip scenarios, 5% to 25%. Furthermore, we calculated 95% confidence intervals for all parameters to determine the reliability and validity of our findings. In these comprehensive Monte Carlo experiments, we assessed critical performance indicators, including average power consumption, accuracy, and corresponding standard deviations. Beyond the global error injection scenarios, we also performed targeted layer-wise fault injections with varying bit-flip rates. Furthermore, in an effort to introduce varying bit-flip fault rates, each layer of the neural network was individually subjected to targeted fault injections. The maximum bit-flip percentage per layer was set at 20%, while the remaining layers remained error-free. This scenario accurately reflects an advanced threat model where an attacker, possessing detailed knowledge of the system workloads and circuit layouts, strategically induces faults. Specifically, we conducted independent fault injection experiments on each of the layers: the first layer at a maximum bit-flip rate of 20%, the second layer at 15%, and the third layer at 10%, as explicitly documented in our simulation datasets. This targeted, layer-specific evaluation quantifies the sensitivity of each individual layer to bit-flip errors, thereby understanding their distinct contributions to the overall inference accuracy degradation. For comprehensive statistical analysis, SPICE-level circuit simulations were conducted, which involved multiple inference assessments on ten unique handwritten digit pictures from the MNIST dataset. Each simulation scenario had five iterative tests for each input image, with predictions rigorously evaluated and documented as either correct or incorrect, facilitating an accurate assessment of accuracy degradation under different bit-flip scenarios. To facilitate dynamic weight mapping effectively, it is essential to identify the crossbar regions vulnerable to PV-induced faults. The IMAC-Sim framework inherently divides DNN layers into smaller sub-arrays to simulate actual hardware environments and interconnect parasitics. Utilizing this sub-array-based framework, we present the VIS metric, a quantitative metric intended to assess fault sensitivity with more precision inside each layer. IMAC-Sim maps DNN weights into MRAM resistive crossbar arrays, whose size directly correlates to each layer’s dimensions from input pixels to output neurons. For the three-layer DNN model evaluated herein, the network uses a 400 × 120 × 84 × 10 architecture. Total weights per layer are computed as follows: $${W}_{layer1}=400\times 120=48,000,\,{W}_{layer2}=120\times 84=10,080,\,{W}_{layer3}=84\times 10=840$$ IMAC-Sim supports multiple sub-array partition configurations (32 × 32, 64 × 64, 128 × 128, and 256 × 256) to effectively mitigate parasitic-induced inaccuracies. Among these, the 32 × 32 configuration yielded optimal accuracy and reliability. Therefore, we employed this configuration to divide each layer into smaller analytical units, termed Fault Observation Windows (FOWs) , enabling precise localized fault sensitivity analyses. Throughout this paper, we use FOW to denote the fixed-size tile produced by IMAC-Sim’s horizontal/vertical partitioning. Thus, “FOW” and “(32 × 32) sub-array tile” are equivalent. We adopt FOW when discussing fault localization, scoring, and mapping. The systematic horizontal ( H p ) and vertical ( V p ) partitioning of each DNN layer into FOWs is formulated as: $${H}_{p}=\left[\frac{{N}_{out}}{{X}_{row}}\right],\,{V}_{p}=\left[\frac{{N}_{in}+1}{{X}_{col}}\right]$$ (4) where N i n and N o u t represent input and output neuron counts for each layer, and X r o w , X c o l denote the row-wise and column-wise dimensions of each FOW (32 × 32 in our scenario). Specifically, for layer 1 (400 inputs vertically and 120 outputs horizontally), the partitioning is: $${V}_{p}=\left[\frac{120}{32}\right]=4,\,{H}_{p}=\left[\frac{400+1}{32}\right]=13$$ Thus, layer 1 is segmented into a total of 4 × 13 = 52 FOWs, indexed systematically in a row-wise manner for targeted fault injection and localized impact analysis. Similarly, the vertical and horizontal partitioning for layer 2 are V p = 3 and H p = 4, and for layer 3 are V p = 1 and H p = 3, respectively. To quantify VIS accurately, each FOW individually undergoes targeted fault injection simulations with randomized bit-flip rates ranging from 50% to 90%. These fault rates translate to as high as 1024 bit-flips per FOW, effectively simulating severe localized faults or targeted adversarial attacks. Compared to system-wide fault injection scenarios, this fault rate remains realistic and manageable when analyzed at the individual FOW granularity. To ensure robustness in VIS estimation, each simulation batch is succeeded by inference on 100 test images, therefore confirming that the measured VIS accurately represents consistent circuit behavior and stability under post-bit-flip conditions. Dynamic weight mapping strategy To enhance the network’s resilience to process-induced variations, we introduce a dynamic mapping strategy that allocates weights from unreliable or degraded regions of the crossbar array to more robust locations. It is important to clarify that while this strategy is primarily a reliability mechanism for mitigating process variation, it also contributes to security hardening by reducing persistent hardware weak points that adversaries could preferentially exploit. Figure 9 provides a conceptual overview of this dynamic mapping strategy. This operation is performed at the granularity of FOWs, the fundamental tiled units of the physical weight matrix, ensuring consistency with the hardware mapping used during SPICE netlist synthesis. The mapping decisions are guided by VIS, which leverages per-FOW performance profiles ( A b , E b ) obtained from a series of targeted fault-injection simulations. These profiles, where A b is the accuracy and E b is the error rate for FOW b , are loaded prior to inference and inform the selection of optimal swap partners. The mapping pipeline is executed independently for each layer’s positive and negative weight matrices to preserve the symmetry of the differential architecture. For a given weight matrix \(W\in {{\mathbb{R}}}^{C\times R}\) , partitioned into a set of N FOWs \(\{{{\mathscr{S}}}_{1},\ldots ,{{\mathscr{S}}}_{N}\}\) , the process begins by identifying the most faulty FOW from VIS. This is achieved by computing the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) between the error-affected matrix W and a clean reference matrix W ref for each FOW: $${{MAE}}_{b}=\frac{1}{| {{\mathscr{S}}}_{b}| }\mathop{\sum }\limits_{(i,j)\in {{\mathscr{S}}}_{b}}| {W}_{ij}-{W}_{{ref},ij}| ,$$ (5) where W i j and W ref, i j are the individual synaptic weights in the error-affected and reference matrices (correct weights), respectively. Also, \(| {{\mathscr{S}}}_{b}| \) is the total number of elements in FOW b . A critical constraint, enforced by the implementation, is that a swap can only occur between FOWs of identical dimensions. In our setting, an FOW of size 32 × 32 may only be swapped with another 32 × 32 tile. Edge tiles created by partitioning must also match exactly: for Layer 1 the valid FOW dimensions are 32 × 32, 32 × 24, 16 × 32, and 16 × 24; for Layer 2: 32 × 32, 32 × 20, 24 × 32, and 24 × 20; and for Layer 3: 32 × 10 and 20 × 10. Swaps across unequal geometries such as 32 × 32 ↔ 32 × 24 are not allowed to avoid line/port mismatches and to preserve the crossbar topology reflected in the SPICE netlist. If no geometry-compatible partner exists within the layer, the mapping for that matrix is skipped, and the next candidate is considered. The selection of k ⋆ is primarily guided by the VIS; candidates are ranked in descending order of their fault-stress accuracy, A k . Ties are broken by selecting the candidate with the lowest average power consumption in regions. Following the selection of a suitable partner, the contents of the faulty FOW \({{\mathscr{S}}}_{{b}^{\star }}\) and the resilient FOW \({{\mathscr{S}}}_{{k}^{\star }}\) are swapped. This physically relocates the high-importance weights from the vulnerable region to a more reliable one without altering the overall crossbar structure or operation count. The effectiveness of this strategy is contingent on the availability of compatible swap partners. In the overall IMAC-Sim flow, this mapping algorithm operates prior to the mapIMAC module, where it loads the necessary weight files and performance metrics, localizes the faulty FOW, selects a VIS-guided partner under strict dimensional constraints, and writes the mapped weight files before the final model is synthesized into a SPICE netlist.
